How speech recognition breaks on bad audio

Listen to a recording before and after it was damaged, then read what seven speech-recognition models wrote. Words that don't match the reference are highlighted.

From the Noisy-Speech ASR Benchmark: 2,000 LibriSpeech recordings × 22 kinds of damage × 7 models, 308,000 transcriptions.

How these clips were chosen: automatically, not by hand. Among the 445 recordings of 7–13 s with 18–35 words, each damage type shows the clip whose per-model error rates are closest to that model's error rate over all 2,000 clips with that damage. For the 500–1000 Hz band, the choice was limited to clips where Whisper tiny loops, which happens on 27% of them. There is no codec example: the Opus encoder gives slightly different output on different machines, so the audio here would not be exactly what the models heard.

What this doesn't show

  • One kind of speech: read English audiobooks (LibriSpeech test-clean), recorded fairly cleanly.
  • The damage is generated, not recorded. The room reverb uses one measured impulse response.
  • Each damage type was generated once, with one seed, and the error rates have no confidence intervals. Small gaps between models are ties, not rankings.
  • All models used greedy decoding through Hugging Face Transformers.

Audio: LibriSpeech (Panayotov et al., 2015), CC BY 4.0, read from LibriVox recordings. The damaged versions were regenerated with the benchmark's own code and seeds, so they are the audio the models heard (checked against the metrics stored for each clip). A clip whose peak would exceed full scale was turned down to fit a 16-bit file.
